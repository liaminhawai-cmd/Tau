Tau · A guide to the game and the evidence

How deep is Tau?

One piece each. One aim: push the other player off the board. How much is there to learn between understanding those rules and playing well?

The evidence so far: Tau has a substantial skill ladder, and its computer players have missed important tactical choices. We have not established how high that ladder goes.

Start with the moves, then examine the evidence. No experience of Tau assumed.

01 · The game

Three feet. A pivot. A choice of where to stop.

Tau is a two-player strategy game on a round board. Each player controls one tripod: a hub with three legs. You take turns moving your own piece. A push can slide and turn the other piece; get one of its feet past the rim and you win.

  1. Pin one foot. Choose which of your three feet stays still.
  2. Swing around it. Turn clockwise or anticlockwise. Contact pushes the opponent as the move unfolds.
  3. Choose where to stop. You may cross one printed line per turn, and your own feet must stay on the board. Within those limits, a move is an arc rather than a jump to a square.

The printed lines limit how far a move can travel. A crossing continues while the piece remains on a line, so rounding a corner can count as one crossing. The game’s “How to play” walkthrough teaches those details interactively.

Where depth could come from: three feet × two directions gives six move families, each with a range of stopping angles. The interesting question is whether choosing among them creates learnable, consequential decisions. A continuous move space alone does not establish that.

03 · The skill ladder

Better players have found more to exploit.

A practical sign of depth is a succession of players that reliably outplay earlier ones. Tau’s hand-built opponents, labelled L1–L11, use different evaluations and search settings. The early tournament found large skill gaps. Training neural-network evaluators subsequently produced players rated above the stronger hand-built opponents.

The live ladder now tests these learned players together with hand-built reference opponents. This is a larger, changing experiment than the original ladder study:

The live self-play league is the current evidence. It is a league of model faces playing one another, including the hand-built reference opponents. The earlier 1,280-game round-robin is useful historical context; these 19,562 games tell us what the current population can separate today.

How to read a rung. Think of jiu-jitsu belts: a blue belt reliably beats a white belt, a purple belt beats a blue belt, and so on. A belt is a useful name for a repeatable gap, not a conversion between sports. In an Elo league, 200 points is a rough “class”: the stronger player is expected to score about 76% of a match. Chess and Go use similar language when people describe skill ranges, but their player pools and rating systems are different. Here we borrow the picture—several meaningful gaps between players—without claiming that a Tau class is literally a chess class.

The earlier report’s “about five chess-classes” shorthand was meant to communicate this rough distance: several repeatable gaps from an inexperienced player to the strongest player we had built. Keep “five” as a scale intuition, not a calibrated Tau-to-chess conversion. The human baseline and a stable top player needed to make that number precise are still missing.

Chess and Go are familiar reference ladders because generations of players have made their ranges legible. Their ratings represent different populations, time controls and cultures of play. The comparison helps a reader picture what a ladder means; the live Tau league supplies the actual current measurement.

The broad signal is that the hand-built players were not the end of the skill ladder. Several learned configurations and the committee sit well above them in this fit. The exact order at the top is much less secure: the highest displayed D1 entry has only four matches, and the live population changes as players are added and retired. The league is therefore strong evidence that the current players differ in ability; it is weaker evidence for a final number of Tau skill levels.

Elo is a relative rating within this league, not a universal measure of game depth. A 200-point gap corresponds to about a 76% expected match score under the rating model. It does not mean a Tau player is equivalent to a chess player with that rating, or that every 200 points is a distinct human skill level.

Why two games make one match. The players swap colours. A 1–1 split is recorded as a drawn match, even when both individual games ended with a winner. The ladder fits match outcomes; its 19,562 game count is not 19,562 independent rating observations.

What this says about depth: the live self-play league now gives us a large, ongoing measurement of relative skill, with several learned faces above the reference ladder. It shows room to climb; it does not yet locate the top or translate Tau directly into chess or jiu-jitsu levels.

04 · The search for a shortcut

Could one simple idea be enough?

Imagine teaching a new player just one rule: “stay in the middle”, “control territory”, or “put your opponent nearest the edge”. If one such rule dominated strong opposition, the game might offer less to learn than its continuous geometry suggests.

An earlier experiment gave single-idea players the same three-move search and let them compete with combined evaluators. Staying central performed badly. Territory and inside-line geometry performed surprisingly well: the former values favourable board regions; the latter keeps your hub between the board’s centre and your opponent’s hub.

This is useful evidence in both directions. The immediate objective is not a sufficient strategy, but a compact positional idea can take a player a long way. The strongest single-idea players traded small wins with the combined evaluator; 7–5 results from 12-game pairings are too thin to establish a lasting strategy cycle.

Do stronger players repeat the same opening?

In the earlier opening study, L8 produced 12 distinct opening sequences in 24 self-play games. L9 produced 21; L10 and L11 each produced 23. Stronger players in this sample did not collapse onto a small opening book.

The starting positions were deliberately perturbed, so variation was partly built into the experiment. Even a solved game can have many equivalent lines, and unsolved play can be repetitive. Opening variety is a description of these players, not a test that rules out a forced opening win.

What this says about depth: the obvious shortcuts have mixed success, and stronger play still produces varied games. That is encouraging, but the strong single-idea results remain a reason to keep testing for simpler explanations.

05 · Search depth versus game depth

Looking further ahead only helps if you consider the right moves.

Search depth counts how many turns a computer examines. Game depth concerns how much there is to master. They are related, but one does not measure the other.

Consider Example A. A search can look several turns ahead yet discard Blue’s useful pivot at the first step because another move has a better immediate score. Extending the remaining branches never recovers the move it omitted.

Try changing the search, not just its length

The maps below hold the evaluator fixed. First compare two and three plies: their scores look almost identical. Then choose “2, wide”, which keeps more candidate moves, or “2, diverse”, which keeps one candidate per foot-and-direction family. The two-ply picture changes substantially.

The narrow two- and three-ply maps correlate at 0.998 in the zoomed region. Yet the narrow and wide two-ply maps correlate at −0.09. Agreement between successive depths was not enough to show that this search had understood the position. The wider maps are not ground truth either.

What does the new ladder add?

The snapshot contains 42 D1, 19 D2, 7 D3 and 5 D4 faces. All active D1 and D2 faces have games; five D3 faces have been measured. Strong entries occur at several depths, while some deeper faces still have no results.

For one concrete comparison, resume-293 appears at +176 in D1 after 76 games and +238 in D2 after 20. That is a promising estimate for the extra search, not a controlled measurement of its benefit: the samples and opponents differ. It is enough to make the old blanket claim that deeper reading “does not pay” inappropriate.

Earlier experiments found modest, noisy search gains: three-ply beat one-ply in 63% of a 24-game comparison, while adjacent depths were hard to separate. Neither those small tests nor a plateau in one training run establish the game’s ceiling. Network size and policy-assisted search also need comparisons that control training, opponents and computation.

What this says about depth: current search has demonstrable blind spots. To measure the value of planning, vary candidate coverage as well as the number of plies, and compare players on the same openings and budget.

06 · Advantage, recovery and balance

How early do the decisions stop mattering?

A game can offer many possible moves but become practically decided very early. Tau’s earlier 1,280-game tournament gives a first look at this question. Games lasted a median of 17 turns; an engine’s estimate of a lead usually predicted better chances, not certainty.

For example, roughly one in four positions in the +16 bucket still belonged to the eventual loser. That can reflect a recoverable position, later mistakes, or an imperfect evaluator. It does not show that every large lead has a defence under best play.

The first move still needs a better measurement

The original mixed-player tournament was close to 50% first-player wins overall. But later L10 and L11 self-play gave the first player 35 wins in 48 games, about 73%. These samples measure different populations. Averaging them together would hide the concern rather than settle it.

The live ladder’s colour-swapped matches make player comparisons fairer, but the many 1–1 results do not by themselves diagnose the opening advantage. That requires game-level outcomes by colour, under a fixed starting setup, with enough games between strong players.

Long games also need care. The original study used a 70-turn cap and reported about 3% reaching it; later long replays often finished. A time cap is an experimental rule, not proof that a position is drawn or that every possible game must terminate.

What this says about depth: observed games leave room for recovery and later decisions. Balance and decisiveness at stronger play are still measurements to make, not settled properties.

07 · The answer so far

A real skill ladder. An unmeasured ceiling.

The strongest case for Tau’s depth is the combination: concrete tactical choices, large gaps between weak and stronger players, and learned players finding improvements beyond the hand-built ladder. None of these alone gives a final number for “how deep”. Together, they give us something substantial to investigate.

What would make the answer sharper?

  1. Measure human improvement. Rate newcomers and experienced players in the same pool, and track what they learn. This would connect engine strength to an actual learning curve.
  2. Run controlled search comparisons. Hold the model and openings fixed; vary stopping resolution, move coverage and search depth. Report both equal-compute performance and the benefit of extra compute.
  3. Retest the leaders. Give promising faces enough colour-balanced matches against a stable reference pool. Follow progress over time, including unsuccessful challengers.
  4. Test balance and tactical claims directly. Measure strong self-play by colour. Search for counterexamples to candidate traps; use rigorous interval or branch bounds where a universal claim is needed.

It is reasonable to call Tau a game with demonstrated tactical and skill depth. It is premature to assign it “five chess-classes”, rank it alongside Go, declare that search has saturated, or infer optimal play from today’s strongest bots. The evidence describes how far our players have climbed; it has not located the top.

08 · Check the evidence

Methods, data and definitions

This guide combines a current ladder readout with results and recorded replays from earlier AI-assisted experiments using Tau’s digital engine. It is not an independent replication or a human tournament study. The rewrite retains the earlier observations while narrowing claims that went beyond them.

The September 2026 ladder: what is being rated?

The supplied snapshot contains 19,562 rated games, 9,781 two-game matches, 73 live model faces and 52 model files. The 73 faces divide into 42 D1, 19 D2, 7 D3 and 5 D4 seats. Six live dual trunks are represented; a dual network has value and policy outputs. “+P” distinguishes its policy-assisted face.

The ten persistent reference opponents are L7–L11 and their corner-opening variants. “Immortal” means they are not culled; it does not mean their Elo is fixed. The current league fits their ratings alongside the learned players.

Retired faces are part of the historical league, so neither 73 nor 52 is a total count of every player tested. The active roster is selected by a training and culling process that also accounts for search cost. The mix of surviving depths is not a controlled experiment.

The rating code fits colour-balanced match outcomes on the usual 400-point Elo scale, with a weak one-match seed prior. In a two-game pairing, 2–0 is a match win, 1–1 a match draw, and 0–2 a loss. The displayed point ratings and interval fields can come from different update paths; this guide does not treat the pasted intervals as current uncertainty bounds for those point estimates.

The committed summary files predate the supplied snapshot. The dated numbers here therefore come from that readout; the source code establishes how to interpret them. No private local dashboard is contacted by this page.

The single-idea round-robin had eight players, 28 unordered pairings and 12 games per pairing: 336 games. All used three-ply search with 9° stop sampling and a 28-candidate setting. The full reported overall win rates were L11 77%, territory 75%, inside-line geometry 75%, L10 67%, immediate score 62%, restricting the opponent 24%, centrality 14% and parking 6%.

A separate 201-position scan found that 82% had a single near-best 9° stopping zone under L11’s one-ply evaluation and a 1.5-unit score tolerance. This measures the sharpness of that evaluator on that grid. It does not establish a unique objectively best move, nor quantify human execution difficulty.

Replays, candidate traps and what counts as a proof

The two examples retain recorded poses from the earlier digital-engine experiments. They play stored trajectories; the page does not run a fresh physics simulation. The introductory slider reuses the escape move. Its recorded frames show both pieces moving, so it is not described here as a contact-free rotation.

The earlier analysis reported four candidate trapped positions from games, 29 proposed throw regions checked at 1,160 random poses without a contradiction, and a small neighbourhood around one candidate checked at 25 poses with eight moves each. Those are useful empirical checks, not exhaustive proofs.

A separate catalogue contained 175 constructed near-rim positions, 137 with an immediate threat; the analysis reported an escape in each of those 137. In another test it found escapes in 38 of 40 positions a narrow two-ply search had called lost. A recorded counterexample can refute a universal loss claim in that model; failure to find a counterexample cannot establish one.

The proposed region checks used sampled finite differences, a safety multiplier and a contact signature. The accompanying mathematical review found that these do not supply the rigorous bounds needed to cover every point between samples. The signature also omits parts of the contact state.

The review’s √7/2 bound applies to an outer-foot radius observable, and 1 + √2 to an own-foot rigid rotation map. Neither is a bound for the entire pushing solver. Very loose bounds for pushed motion do not prove that useful local certification is impossible. A rigorous contact-free entry calculation would still need a rigorously established target region before it could prove a forced win.

Likewise, retro-mining positions that none of the current player pool could escape, sometimes many turns before the end, identifies useful training challenges. It does not prove a forced sequence against every legal defence.

A short glossary
Move family
A pinned foot and a rotation direction; different stopping angles give different moves within it.
Throw
A foot passes the engine’s boundary threshold. The replay gauge includes its small numerical rim allowance.
Evaluation
A player’s estimate of how favourable a position is. It can be wrong.
Ply / D1–D4
One turn of lookahead / search settings that examine one to four plies.
Candidate list
The subset of sampled moves a search retains for further examination.
Face
A model combined with a search or policy setting. Several faces can share one network.
Committee
A player combining several evaluators’ judgements.
Forced win
A strategy that wins against every legal response. Sampled success alone does not establish it.
Elo
A relative fit to match outcomes within a player pool. The zero is arbitrary.
