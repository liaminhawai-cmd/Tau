Tau  ·  How deep is it?  ·  Fourth edition

How many levels of play does Tau have, and how does that compare with chess and Go?

Tau is a two-player game with one piece each: a tripod that you pin on one foot and swing, crossing at most one line per turn, until you shove the other piece off a round board. Since the game shipped we have played it against itself well over a hundred thousand times, built a ladder of eleven computer opponents, trained learned players from 19 thousand to 2 million parameters on millions of positions, and built a proof engine that can certify whether a given position is lost. This report is the one place all of that is put together, for someone who has never played: what we asked, what we found, how sure we are, and what we would do next.

Verdict:  Tau already has about five chess-classes of skill between a newcomer and the best player we have built, every level found so far has been beaten by the next, no forced line exists at any strength we can reach, and the ceiling is set by our players, not by the game. That is the shape of a deep game. How deep is still being measured.

Produced with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant, running the game’s own engine. This is an AI analysis of computer play, checked against the game's code and data. It is not an independent human study, and it says so wherever that matters.

Start here

What depth means, and how you measure it in a game nobody has played for long.

People say a game is deep when getting better at it never seems to end. Chess and Go are the reference points: centuries of play, millions of players, and still no book that tells you the best move. Both have something you can count. Line the players up so that each one beats the one below about three games in four, and see how many rungs the ladder has. In chess that ladder runs from a beginner to the world champion in roughly eleven such steps, and the strongest engines add three or four more. Go's runs about twice as long. Draughts and Othello, both strong games, run shorter. The number is not the whole story, but it is the part you can put in a table.

Tau has no centuries and no millions. It has a few months, one lab, and a game engine that can play itself very fast. So the question this report can answer is narrower and more honest than "is Tau as deep as Go": how many rungs of skill have we been able to find so far, is anything about the game stopping the ladder from growing, and what does play look like at the top of what we have? Everything below is organised around those three questions, and each section ends by saying how sure we are.

A word on the pieces. Blue and Red each have one tripod. A move is a choice of which foot to pin, which way to turn, and where to stop, and the stop can be any angle, so a Tau position is continuous rather than a grid. The only constraint is that a swing may cross one printed line. When a swing runs into the other piece it shoves it, and the shove is real physics, resolved as the arc goes. You lose the moment one of your feet is past the rim. That is the whole rulebook, and it is why several things measured here have no chess or Go equivalent.

Question one

How many levels does Tau have? So far, about five chess-classes above a newcomer, and the top keeps moving.

Think of jiu-jitsu belts: a blue belt beats a white belt, purple beats blue, brown beats purple, black beats brown, and a competition black belt beats an ordinary one. Each belt is a real, repeatable gap. Rating systems make that idea precise. In chess, a gap of 200 Elo points means the stronger player scores about 76%, and 200 points is called a class. Counting classes between a beginner and the best player is the standard way to say how long a game's ladder is.

Tau's ladder has been built rung by rung, and every rung was tested against the one below it before it shipped. The game offers eleven computer opponents. Levels 1 and 2 are hand rules; Level 3 is the first that plans a move; Level 6 is the first that reads the reply; Level 7 reads its own follow-up; Levels 8 and 9 add finer choices of where to stop the swing; Levels 10 and 11 keep Level 9's search and change only what the brain values, with weights learned from the game's own data. Head to head, the upper steps measure 83, 63, 67, 71 and 67% for the stronger level from Level 6 up to Level 11: a real staircase, no step skippable, no step a wall. On the ladder's own rating scale that is roughly 940 Elo from Level 1 to Level 11.

Above Level 11 sit the learned players. Neural networks trained on self-play, searching only one or two moves ahead, now rate 200 to 270 Elo above Level 11 when measured over forty to eighty games each, and a committee of three brains voting together rates about 360 above it. The very top of the live table reads a little higher still, on samples too small to trust. Nothing we have built has failed to be beaten by something built later.

Where do people fit? We do not yet have a rated human pool, so this is informal: in play so far, most first-timers get stuck around Level 4, and the strongest human games on record beat Level 11, using an opening idea (rounding a printed corner to cross two lines on one crossing budget) that the engines had to be taught. Take a newcomer at Level 4 and the best learned player as the top, and the ladder is about 1,000 Elo long: about five chess-classes, or nine or ten belts at the ladder's own 65% spacing. How that compares. The table is the honest comparison, and the caveat matters more than the numbers: chess's and Go's ladders are what centuries, huge talent pools and superhuman engines have found. Tau's is what one lab found in a few months with a shipped engine and a training rig. A count of five is a floor, and every edition of this report has raised it (Level 8 was the top in the first edition; Level 11 in the second; the nets in the third). The right reading is not "Tau is half as deep as chess" but "we have found five classes and have not yet hit anything that looks like a bottom".

How sure: the ladder's ordering and adjacent win rates are solid (24 to 60 games per step, re-verified under the current rules). The Elo span is soft by perhaps a class either way, because Tau's ladder is not perfectly transitive (an aggressive Level 2 beats the cautious Levels 3 to 5) and the learned players are rated in a different pool from the shipped levels. The human placements are anecdote until the next study.

Question two

As players get better, does Tau collapse onto one line? No. Stronger play gets more varied.

The warning sign of a shallow game is convergence: once players are good enough, everyone plays the same few moves and the game feels solved. We have now tested this at seven strengths across three editions. Each level plays itself 24 times from tiny random starting nudges, and we count how many genuinely different opening lines come out (which foot pivots and which way it turns, ignoring exact angles).

A solved game lands near the bottom of the chart: one or two lines, repeated. Tau does the opposite, and the trend runs the wrong way for a shallow game. The weakest level is varied because it is random. The middle levels narrow a little, as they do in chess. Then every stronger player found since has widened play again: Level 8 produced 12 distinct lines in 24 games; the same brain given more search produced 17; Level 9 produced 21; Levels 10 and 11 produced 23 each, with the single most common line appearing in only 1 in 12 games. No full game ever repeated another: 72 of 72 top-level games had distinct move sequences.

How sure: solid on direction, modest on precision (24 games per level). This is the finding that has survived the most attempts to break it, including giving the top brain far more search and looking for repeated games.

Question three

Does a lead settle the game, and does going first? A lead helps. Neither decides it.

Depth also lives in whether a lead is safe. Pooling every position from 1,280 ladder games, we measured how often the player ahead by a given amount actually went on to win. From a dead-even position the eventual winner is a coin flip (47%). A real, visible lead is worth about 75%, so one game in four is still lost from there. A commanding lead reaches about 85%. Only 0.9% of games reached a point where the result was locked in before the closing moves. Games run a median of 17 turns with a long tail, and the few very long ones are slow positions, not stalemates: replayed with the turn cap lifted, the strongest levels finished every game.

Going first is a small edge at most. Across all 1,280 ladder games the first player won exactly 50%; in level-versus-itself play it averaged 41%, a slight edge to the responder. At the top of the ladder the edge turns the other way: about two-thirds at Level 8 and 73% pooled over 48 games at Levels 10 and 11. That is the ordinary edge strong abstract games carry (chess, Go and Hex all favour the opener a little), and nowhere near a forced win. Because Tau's start is continuous, it also has a balance dial discrete games lack: if the edge holds up at higher strength, the second player can be compensated by a few degrees of starting rotation, a komi measured in degrees, without touching a rule.

How sure: the comeback curve is descriptive and pools positions across games, so its buckets are not independent samples; the shape is robust, the third decimal is not. The first-mover figures at Levels 10 and 11 rest on 48 games and should be re-measured with the learned players.

Question four

Where does the difficulty live? In judgement and the precise stop, not in long calculation.

Chess rewards calculation: reading concrete lines many moves deep. Go rewards judgement: evaluating shapes no one can read out. Which is Tau? Three separate lines of evidence give the same answer, and it is the least expected result in the study.

Reading deeper pays little once judgement is good. One brain, one sampling, three search depths: three-ply beat greedy only 63% end to end, and the adjacent steps were noise. Pushing the top brain's search finer than its shipped setting lost games; a wider beam gained nothing. Meanwhile re-learning what the brain values at the same depth was worth a full rung (77%), and a single new geometric idea (owning the inside line between the centre and the opponent) was worth another (67%). Across 77 learned evaluators the pattern is exact: the worse a net judges positions, the more a second ply gives it (correlation −0.62), and the strongest nets gain nothing from a second or third ply at all. Part of the reason is physical: a Tau move is a full sweep with its pushes and crossings resolved as it goes, so even a one-ply player is doing a kind of lookahead a chess one-ply search is not.

No single idea is safe, and the score is not the strategy. Six bots each optimising one idea, all given the top level's full search, played a 336-game round-robin against the shipped brains. The bot optimising the actual win condition, rim safety, finished mid-table. Two ideas (territory, the inside line) played at the level of the best combined brains; three were useless alone; and the three strongest brains in the study beat each other in a circle, each by the same modest edge. At the ceiling there is no best way to play, only answers to answers, which is the working definition of a metagame. The unflattering half of this is reported straight: at three plies, one well-chosen idea can reach this population's top, a narrowness signal at the engines' depth.

Each position usually has one best move, but the sequence never repeats. Scanning 201 strong-play positions, about 82% had a single nine-degree stopping zone that was near-optimal, not a range. That is chess-like sharpness, not Go's several live candidates. Tau's depth does not live in per-turn breadth; it lives in the fact that those single-best replies still do not collapse into a repeated line (the previous section), and in the precision the stop demands: certified winning entries into some traps need the stop within about half a degree, and the outcome of real games has turned on less than a hundredth of a degree.

The engines' own maps show whose boundaries they are. Scoring a position for every place Red's piece could stand gives a danger map. Below, one region of the board at Level 3 looking one, two and three moves ahead: the picture changes completely from ply 1 to ply 2 (correlation −0.40) and then stops changing (0.998 from ply 2 to ply 3, 0.99 across the whole board at the top level). It looks like convergence. It is not: drawn again with a wider list of candidate moves, the two-ply map is a different picture (correlation −0.09 with the narrow one), and a third searcher that keeps one candidate per pivot-and-direction agrees with the wide one (0.59) and with ply 1 (0.67) but not with the narrow one (−0.10). The shipped searcher's two-ply map is the odd one out, and ply-on-ply agreement was that searcher agreeing with itself. The lines on these maps belong to the searcher's candidate list, not to the game, which is exactly what the proofs in the next section find at the scale of single positions.

How sure: the direction (knowledge over search) has now been seen on hand-built brains, on learned brains and on maps, three independent ways, at every strength we have. What remains open is whether a fundamentally different searcher, one that plans over the continuous move space rather than a candidate list, would change it. Nothing built so far does.

Question five

What is actually proven? Forced losses exist, they are rare endgame traps, and the engines misjudge them.

Everything above is statistical. A tendency cannot settle whether a particular position is lost, so the third edition built the tool that can. The engine's collision rule was reduced to a three-line physical law (a minimum-motion push that trades slide for spin by the contact's lever arm), and that law reproduces the shipped engine's pushes to within its rounding. With a law you can take one position and certify that every move the player has ends with a foot past the rim.

Mining the recorded games for positions where the loser had no escape produced four positions from real play in which the side to move is dead: every legal swing, at every stopping angle, is followed by a throw. Each was certified by the law and then attacked by the engine with hundreds of random moves without finding an escape. Around one of them a whole region was certified dead, and twelve families of positions one move earlier were certified as forced wins in two. The animation replays one of them.

They are rare, and they need contact. About 7% of lost games passed through a provably dead position one move before the end, and none did two moves before. In 175 constructed near-rim positions where the pieces were not touching, a throw was available in 137, and the threatened piece, moving first, escaped in all 137. A dead position in Tau is a piece already jammed against its opponent at the rim, not a piece merely standing near the edge.

The engines see forced losses that are not there. Of 40 positions the shipped two-ply search marked as lost, 38 had an escape, each a quiet re-orientation the search never examined because its candidate list is filled by score and quiet moves score badly. Widening the list cut the false verdicts by more than half. So part of the "narrowness" measured in earlier editions was the searchers' narrowness, not the game's, and the retro-mining that feeds the trainer now routinely finds positions lost for every brain in a pool of sixty from three to fifteen moves before the end: long forced sequences exist below the engines' horizon.

How sure: the certificates are sampled claims checked by the engine, not theorems; an independent mathematical review confirmed that strict proof of the pushes is out of reach and adopted floors for the allowances. Four dead points from real games is a small set. The appendix has the method and every table.

Question six

What did the learning machines find? The ceiling moved, capacity barely matters, and knowledge still pays.

Since the second edition a training rig has run on two machines around the clock: self-play in batches (past 125 batches on each), a league in which every learned player and every shipped level are rated against each other (19,500 rated games at the time of writing), a promotion gate that requires a new champion to beat the old one in eighty colour-balanced games, and retro-mining of lost games to teach the nets the traps. The repository's data snapshot holds about 130,000 distinct games and 2.6 million positions; more than 77 learned evaluators have been rated, at network sizes from 19 thousand to 2.06 million parameters, a hundredfold range.

The ceiling moved and then slowed. The first nets that beat Level 11 arrived within weeks. The best-rated single nets now sit 200 to 270 Elo above it and a committee of three about 360 above. But promotions have become occasional rather than routine: in most recent cycles every challenger measures within noise of the champion (the gate at cycle 392 read +0 ± 78 Elo for the closest), with a new champion only every hundred cycles or so. Whether that is a plateau of the method (one-ply nets on a fixed feature set) or a ceiling of the game is the central open question of this edition, and the honest answer is that nothing yet distinguishes the two.

Capacity buys loudness, not resolution. Nets a hundred times larger did not see finer structure: three two-million-parameter nets agree with each other closely on the fine texture of the map (0.88 to 0.96) and only weakly with Level 11 (0.24 to 0.38), and in play a net of 185 thousand parameters rated slightly above the two-million champion, a 35-thousand net level with it, and only the smallest nets, around 20 thousand parameters, fell away (−130 to −220 Elo). A policy head (a move-suggesting output) has so far been worth nothing measurable. Three different brains voting together rate about a hundred Elo above the best single net in the table, which says the brains disagree in useful ways, another mark of a game with more than one good idea in it.

How sure: league ratings carry confidence intervals of roughly ±80 Elo at forty games, so individual placements shift week to week; the gaps quoted are those with the most games behind them. The game count is from the data snapshot in the repository and undercounts the second machine.

Next studies

What would move the number.

In order of how much each would change this report.

  1. A human ladder. The game already records, per level, how many players have beaten it, and ranked online play has an Elo. Neither has been analysed. Fitting humans onto the same scale as the levels would replace every "informal" above with a number, and tell us whether human improvement stalls where the engines' does.
  2. A different kind of player. Every brain so far chooses from a candidate list of stops. A planner that searches the continuous move space directly (or a learned policy that proposes stops) is the one thing that could either extend the ladder past the current top or show that it ends there. This is also the test that would settle whether the slowing of the champion is a plateau of the method.
  3. The ceiling, on purpose. Train past the current champion with the promotion gate in place and record every cycle: if the Elo curve bends flat while the maps keep changing, that is evidence of a ceiling in our method, not the game; if both go flat together, it is the game.
  4. Komi in degrees. Measure the first-mover edge with the learned players over a few hundred games, and if it holds near two-thirds, find the starting rotation that evens it.
  5. Grow the proofs. Extend the certified dead regions (currently thin) and mine deeper for positions lost two or more moves before the end, so the "rare traps" claim rests on more than four seeds.
  6. The physical game. The rig measures the digital engine. A tournament on the physical set would test whether the precision axis (stopping within a degree) survives real hands and real magnets.

How this was measured

Method, in plain terms.

Everything here comes from the game's real engine playing itself, not a simplified model. The same code that runs the game ran every tournament, the training league and the proof engine.

At a glance

Editions
First: 1,280 ladder games (eight levels, every pairing, both colours, 20 games each) plus opening-variety runs. Second: 900+ games under the current rules, the shallowness battery, Levels 9 to 11. Third: the contact law and certificates, danger maps, the learned population. Fourth: this synthesis, with the ladder count and the comparison.
Players

Starting positions

Ratings
Elo with the usual 400-point scale. The shipped levels' ratings come from round-robins among themselves; the learned players are rated in a live league with the levels as fixed anchors. The two pools do not share a zero, which is why spans are quoted as ranges.
Comparison figures
Chess and Go class counts are approximate conversions from their public rating systems, given for orientation. Tau's count is a measurement of our players, not of the game's ceiling.
Honest caveats
Computer play only, no rated humans yet. Adjacent-level samples are 24 to 60 games. Certificates are sampled claims checked by the engine, not theorems. Because Tau's state is continuous, self-play can fail to find a forced line but cannot prove none exists.

The engine and the tournament

Every game was played by the shipping game's own code, not a re-implementation or a simplified model. A turn is driven synchronously: the active brain returns a plan, the piece is rotated in 3-degree steps until it reaches that plan's stop angle or the rules halt it, and the turn ends. The physics is deterministic, so a chosen plan replays to exactly the position the brain searched.

To stop the near-deterministic top brains from playing the identical game every time, each game began from the standard start with an independent random rotation of up to ±6° applied to each piece. That keeps every position a real, legal Tau position while giving genuinely different lines. Games ran to a piece leaving the board, or to a 70-turn safety cap (reached in 2.9% of games), after which the piece with the better rim margin was recorded as ahead.

The full tournament was every ordered pairing of the eight levels (64 pairings, both colours getting the first move), 20 games each, for 1,280 games. A separate 72-game run (24 self-play games at three levels) measured opening variety, and the stress tests below added more.

A note on rules builds. Between the first edition and the second-edition battery, the game's crossing rule was tightened twice (the leave-a-line grace band went from pad scale to magnet scale, and corner crossings gained a deferred validity check) after two exploits were found and closed. The 1,280-game base tournament predates that tune; every second-edition test ran under the current rules. The change closes two specific cheap tricks; it does not alter the objective, the move set, or the board. We re-verified the ladder's ordering under the current rules with fresh adjacent-rung head-to-heads before extending it, and the one rung the retune had silently weakened (L8's park aim) was repaired before any new measurement was taken.

The eight brains, one by one

Six of the eight share one planner. A move is a triple: which of the three feet is pinned, which way it rotates, and the stop angle. The planner sweeps each of the six (pivot × direction) options in 3° steps until the one-crossing rule or a 170° safety cap stops it, scores candidate stopping points with a weighted evaluation of the resulting position, and, on the deeper rungs, searches the opponent's reply and its own follow-up. Two brains are deliberate exceptions. Elo figures come from a roughly 600-game round-robin; each rung is tuned to beat the one below it about 60 to 65% of the time.

L1 · Pushover Elo 0 (baseline)

kind: l1

Pins the foot nearest the opponent, swings the farthest foot toward them, but stops at a random 45 to 80% of the legal limit and often halts before even the first crossing. One move in five picks a random pivot instead. It gives no thought to its own safety. Built to be beatable by a complete first-timer.

L2 · Menu “Easy” Elo ~117

kind: builtin · dumb heuristic · no lookahead, no evaluation

One fixed rule. If it sits closer to the centre than the opponent it attacks (pin the foot second-farthest from the opponent, swing the farthest foot toward them); otherwise it defends with the same shape aimed at the centre. It always stops a random 60 to 90% short of the natural limit, so it never fully commits.

L3 · Cautious pusher Elo ~200

kind: p · 1-ply · sampleDeg 26 · keepStops 1 · w: margin .6 · noise .30

The first brain on the shared planner: one ply, coarse 26° sampling, scored on rim safety alone. A weakness dial makes it play a purely random legal move 30% of the time. Diluting a competent brain this way reads as more human than a brain that simply under-commits.

L4 · Centre-dancer Elo ~273

kind: p · 1-ply · sampleDeg 18 · keepStops 2 · w: margin .5, zone .3, center 1.5

Finer 18° sampling, still one ply, but now valuing board control: rim safety, the hand-graded cell values, and a strong bonus for keeping a foot near the middle. That centre bonus is what produces its dramatic full-board swings.

L5 · Corner-cutter Elo ~323

kind: p · 1-ply · sampleDeg 18 · keepStops 2 · w: margin .6, zone .4, park 2

Adds the park term: a bonus for resting a foot on a line near a corner intersection, arming it to cut a second line next turn. It begins setting up the two-lines-for-one-crossing trick, though it still does not look at the reply.

L6 · Solid Elo ~392

kind: p · 2-ply · sampleDeg 24 · keepStops 1 · maxCands 8 · w: margin 1, triMe .1

The first brain that looks ahead. For each sampled move it lets the opponent answer with their best sweep-to-limit reply and scores by the worst case (minimax). Rim safety plus a touch of the inside-line triangle term (see L11), added later to even the gap to L7 and give L6 a personality of its own.

L7 · Expert Elo ~461

kind: builtin · master · 3-ply minimax · ladder weights zone 2.5, park 2.2, oppFree −.3

Full three-ply search: its move, the opponent's best reply, then its own best follow-up, so it will not take a move that looks good now but walks into a bad answer. Its move set adds early park stops that deliberately arm a foot in a line's grace band, letting the deeper plies see and race the double-cross. Evaluation is margin plus zone plus a park bonus, with the win/loss term dominating. The ladder's copy carries a light dose of the self-play-fitted weights (the re-spread described below); the vs-Computer menu's Expert keeps the stock constants.

L8 · Cap Elo ~550 (Expert +90)

kind: p3 · 3-ply · sampleDeg 18 · keepStops 2 · maxCands 11 · w: margin 1, zone .5, park .9, center .15

Expert's three-ply shape, but the root is built from fine 18° partial-rotation stops rather than only sweep-to-limit moves, so it weighs stopping points Expert never considers, under the fullest weight set (all four positional terms). The strongest opponent the game shipped when the main tournament was run.

L9 · Deep est. Elo ~670

kind: p3 · 3-ply · sampleDeg 9 · keepStops 4 · maxCands 28 · w: margin 1, zone 2.2, park 1.9, center .12, oppFree −.2

L8's brain given roughly 2.5× the search: half the sample spacing, double the kept stops, 28 candidate lines searched in full. This is the L8+ stress-test player from the appendix below, later shipped as a real rung, and its weights now carry a half dose of the self-play-fitted eval so the ladder's steps stay even.

L10 · Refit est. Elo ~820

kind: p3 · same search as L9 · w: margin 1, zone 12, park 8, oppFree −1.1

L9's exact search with the evaluation re-learned from data: logistic regression over thousands of labeled self-play positions, tested on held-out games. The fit promoted zone and park hard, deleted the centre bonus outright (it predicted losses once territory was priced), and added oppFree, a boxing-in term that shrinks the opponent's room to their nearest line. Beat the shipping L9 46 of 60 (77%).

L11 · Triangle est. Elo ~940 (current ceiling)

kind: p3 · same search as L10 · w: L10 + triMe .2

L10 plus one new idea: triMe, the angle at the piece's own hub between the board centre and the opponent's hub. A wide angle means the piece owns the inside line between the centre and its opponent. It was the strongest single predictor found in a second feature-research round and carried a full rung on its own: L11 beat L10 head-to-head 24 of 36 (67%) at identical search cost.

What the brains value

Positions are scored by a weighted sum of these terms. Weights are the per-level dials listed above; a winning or losing position dominates every positional term.

margin
Rim safety: the opponent's outermost foot clearance from the edge minus mine. Positive means the opponent is nearer to falling. This is the core objective and it decides close calls.
zone
Hand-graded cell value: 4 in the centre, 3 mid, 2 outer, minus 1 inside either corner lens pocket. Summed over my three feet minus the opponent's.
center
Bonus for keeping a foot near the board's middle, which drives large board-crossing swings.
park
Bonus per foot resting in a line's grace band near an intersection, ready to cross a second line next turn (the double-cross setup).
oppFree
The opponent's summed room to their nearest line, clamped per foot. Weighted negative: shrinking it herds their feet against lines so their sweeps hit the one-crossing budget sooner. Learned from self-play data (L9 to L11).
triMe
The angle at my hub between the board centre and the opponent's hub, in degrees. Wide = I own the inside line between the centre and them. The strongest single predictor found in the feature research (L6, L11).
noise
Probability of ignoring the evaluation entirely and playing a random legal move. The weakness dial for the lower rungs.

The deeper-search stress test (L8+)

The one worry the main report cannot fully close is subtle: what if the game only looks varied because even L8 is not strong enough to find a hidden best line, and a deeper search would collapse onto it? To probe that, we built L8+: the exact same evaluation as L8 (identical weights, identical judgement) given a much wider, finer search: half the sample spacing, double the kept stops, and roughly 2.5 times as many candidate lines searched in full. This is more thinking time, not a different player.

First we confirmed the extra search actually makes it stronger, so the test is meaningful: L8+ beat stock L8 in 16 of 24 head-to-head games (67%). It is a genuinely better player, not just a different one. Then the two depth questions:

Opening variety went up, not down. Playing itself from perturbed starts, L8+ produced 17 distinct opening lines out of 24 games with its single most-common line appearing only 8% of the time, more varied than stock L8 (12 of 24, top line 21%). A deeper search found more good ideas, not one forced line.

No forced line at any depth. Playing itself 30 times from perturbed starts, L8+ produced 30 distinct games: no full move sequence ever repeated another. Its median game ran 21 turns. Going first was worth about two-thirds here (20 of 30), the same mild first-mover edge stock L8 shows at the top of the ladder, and nowhere near a forced win.

This was the strongest single piece of evidence in the first edition of this report. The exact failure a publisher would fear, that strength hides a degenerate line, is where the game held up best: give the top brain far more search and it fans out rather than narrowing.

Postscript: L8+ proved strong enough that it has since shipped as Level 9, and the research that followed produced Levels 10 and 11 above it. The second-edition battery below re-runs the convergence and determinism tests at the new ceiling.

Termination: does the cut-off hide draws?

Games in the main tournament ran to a piece leaving the board or a 70-turn cap, and 2.9% hit the cap. A fair worry is that the cap converts genuine stalemates into recorded wins. We checked two ways.

First, we classified all 37 capped games by their margin trajectory. Only 4 (0.3% of all 1,280 games) were near-even deadlocks with neither side progressing. The median capped game held a rim-margin lead of 15, a clear leader that simply had not delivered the final push. And 22 of the 37 were a level playing itself, 10 of those the pure-defence L6 against its clone, exactly the matchup that should grind.

The shallowness battery, in full

Every test below was designed to find shallowness, not to showcase depth: each one encodes a specific way an abstract game can be secretly simple, and asks whether Tau is. All games ran under the current rules with the usual ±6° start perturbation and 70-turn cap.

The cycle is exact: zone beats L11, L11 beats triMe, triMe beats zone, each 7–5, and all three beat L10 cleanly. Twelve-game edges are individually weak evidence, but the structure matters: in this population there is no dominant brain. Pure territory play is punished by inside-line play, inside-line play is punished by the combined evaluation, and the combined evaluation is punished by pure territory. Every fixed strategy at the ceiling has a counter, which is the working definition of a metagame. Notably the strongest bots were also the hardest to separate (7–5s at the top against 12–0s at the bottom), the same compression real game populations show as skill rises.

Test 3 · Are the strategic variables real, or one thing renamed? (6,086 positions)

If the eval's six terms secretly measured one quantity, combining them would be theatre. Measured across 6,086 positions from 180 games: margin, zone and triMe do form a correlated cluster (r = 0.66 to 0.87), they are three lenses on "who stands better", while park, center and oppFree are nearly orthogonal to it and to each other (|r| ≤ 0.19). As a static readout of who is winning, one lens is nearly enough: zone alone scores a held-out AUC of 0.770 against 0.785 for all six combined. But Test 1 shows the flip side: bots that only differ in which correlated lens they optimise (margin-only vs zone-only) are separated by 13 points of win rate, and the near-orthogonal support terms, useless alone, are part of why the combined brains convert wins the single-idea bots cannot. Easy to score, harder to play: the judgement and the strategy are not the same object.

Test 4 · Does reading ahead pay? (72 games)

One brain (L11's evaluation), one sampling (9°), three search depths: greedy (take the best-looking stop), 2-ply (consider the reply), 3-ply (and the follow-up), 24 games per pairing. End to end, depth pays modestly: 3-ply beat greedy 63%. The adjacent steps were statistical noise (2-ply vs greedy 46%, 3-ply vs 2-ply 50%). Two things are true at once here. It is another narrowness signal at the engines' level, reported as such. And it sharpens where Tau's difficulty lives: a Tau "move" is not a quiet board update but a full physical sweep, pushes and crossings resolved as it goes, so even the greedy player is doing a kind of lookahead a chess one-ply search is not. The one large search dividend ever banked on this ladder (L8's 18° sampling to L9's 9°, worth 67%) came from finer stopping choices along the sweep, not from deeper reading, which is consistent with where Test 6 finds the game's texture. Meanwhile refitting judgement at fixed depth was worth 77%. In Tau, judgement beats calculation.

Test 5 · Do the new champions converge? (72 games)

The first edition's biggest worry was that a stronger player would collapse onto a narrow book. The three newest levels each played themselves 24 times from perturbed starts: L9 produced 21 distinct openings of 24 (top line 8%), L10 and L11 each 23 of 24 (top lines 8%), all three more varied than L8's 12 of 24. Every increase in strength so far has widened play, not narrowed it, now confirmed across four successively stronger brains. No full game ever repeated: 72 of 72 distinct fingerprints. One number moved the other way and is reported straight: the first-mover edge at the two newest levels ran 18/24 and 17/24 (73% pooled), above the roughly two-thirds seen at L8. The samples are small, but if the trend holds at higher strength Tau has a balance dial discrete games lack: its start is continuous, so the second player can be compensated by degrees of starting rotation, a komi measured in degrees, without touching a rule.

Test 6 · How many good moves does a position hold? (201 positions)

Go's texture is famous: most turns offer several defensible moves. To measure Tau's, we scanned strong-play positions (L11 self-play, turn three onward), scored every 9° stop of all six (pivot × direction) move families with L11's evaluation, and asked how tightly the near-optimal set converges. It converges hard. About 90% of positions have a single move family within tolerance; drilling into that family, 82% have a single 9° stopping zone that is near-optimal, not a range, and the near-optimal stops straddle a line-crossing or a park boundary in only 2% of positions. So a strong-play Tau position is sharply peaked: usually one best move, direction and stopping zone together, with no near-equal tactical alternative. An earlier draft of this report called the stop "a rich continuous choice"; the measurement above corrected that, and this section reports the sharper result.

This is a real narrowness finding, and it is the least Go-like thing about Tau, so it is worth being exact about what it does and does not mean. It is not a shallowness verdict: almost every decisive tactical game has one clearly-best move per position under a sharp evaluator, chess included; Go's several-live-candidates texture is the outlier, not the norm. Tau's depth does not live in per-turn breadth. It lives in the sequence: those near-forced single-best replies still fail to collapse into a repeated line (openings stay varied and widen with strength, Test 5), because different positions demand different single moves. Two honest qualifiers: the scan is a static 1-ply positional readout at 9° resolution and a 1.5-unit tolerance, so the full-depth search value could tie more stops than this shows; and hitting that one precise stop under a continuous drag is itself a real-time precision skill, a different axis from move selection that a continuous-control game carries and a grid game does not.

Test 7 · Is the ceiling real, or does it keep receding?

A solved-ish game runs out of new ideas: past some point, neither more search nor more knowledge buys wins. Tau's two axes currently behave very differently. Search saturated: pushing the top brain's sampling finer than 9° (7°, 40 candidates) actually lost head-to-heads (44%), and a wider beam at 9° gained nothing (58%, within noise) — more thinking time alone stopped paying. Knowledge did not: refitting the evaluation's weights from self-play data was worth a full rung (Level 10, 77% over Level 9), a single new geometric idea was worth another (Level 11, 67% over Level 10), and this battery immediately found two more brains that beat L10 in turn. One measured-but-unused idea is already banked: the opponent's real swept-arc width predicts wins out of sample but costs six engine sweeps per evaluated position, waiting on a faster engine. A game whose evaluation keeps admitting new ideas, while raw search has gone flat, is bounded by our understanding, not by its own bottom.

So is it as deep as Go? What this battery can and cannot say.

Honestly: no self-play study with hand-built three-ply bots can certify parity with a game whose depth is proven by three thousand years of still-deepening human theory and by the fact that mastering it required superhuman compute. What this battery can do is measure the signatures that separate deep games from shallow ones, and try hard to trigger the shallow ones. The scorecard: no forced win found at any strength and opening variety that rises with search (deep-game signature, both editions); a first-move edge that stays mild everywhere (healthy range, alongside chess and Go); essentially no draws against chess's high and rising draw rate at strength; leads that never lock the game (comeback room); the objective displaced from the strategy (Test 1); a strategy cycle rather than a best answer at the ceiling (Test 2); a receding knowledge ceiling (Test 7). Set against that: a single static idea can reach this population's ceiling (Test 1), the strategic readout is low-dimensional (Test 3), reading deeper adds little once judgement is good (Test 4), and a strong-play position converges sharply on one best move, direction and stopping zone together (Test 6), so Tau's per-turn texture is chess-like sharpness rather than Go's several live candidates, with its depth carried by the sequence of those single-best moves not by per-turn breadth. The fair summary is that Tau passes, at its current engine strength, every falsification test we could construct, several of them designed specifically to catch a game that merely looks varied, and the two tests it does not pass cleanly are bounded by the engines' depth, not by evidence of a degenerate core. The next tier of evidence would need a fundamentally stronger player (tree search over the continuous move space, or a learned policy) and human tournament data. Both are noted as open work, not assumed.

The third-edition certificates, in full

The contact law: the moving piece is a rigid rotation about its pinned foot; the pushed piece is a free rigid body in the plane with mass 1 and moment of inertia 0.7 R² (R the leg reach); at each contact the correction is the smallest motion that removes the overlap, which splits into a slide and a spin in the ratio set by the contact's lever arm; no friction, no bounce. Run at the engine's own step (0.4° substeps, ten solver passes, a penetration cap of 0.8u) it reproduces the engine's pushed pose to 0.000u on 200 recorded collisions; run as an ideal law (0.1° steps, unlimited passes) it differs from the engine by 0.02u at the median and 0.63u at worst, all of the difference traced to the engine's 12-segment leg polylines and its step size.

A certificate here is a sampled claim: the throw margin (how far past the rim the victim's outermost foot travels, positive means thrown) is evaluated on a grid over a region of poses; the steepest finite-difference slope between neighbouring samples, times three, times half a cell, is the allowance; a region is certified thrown when every sample clears its allowance and the contact signature (which leg pairs touch, in which order, up to the throw) is the same across it; regions where the signature changes are refused rather than averaged. Every certified region is then attacked by the engine with random poses and random moves, and a single contradiction voids it. None occurred. A dead point is a position where the side to move has every legal reply (six pivot-direction arms, every stopping angle to the rule's limit, the pushes played out by the law) followed by a certified throw. A dead ball extends that to every position within a radius, using the same slope allowance over the twelve axis probes.

The mathematical review. The certificate method was put to an independent mathematical review with the engine source. Its findings, adopted here: the sampled rule is not a proof (its hypotheses are not supplied by a signature match and a factor of three); a first contact has sensitivity of norm one, so grazing is not the source of jumps; the outermost-foot switch is an exact kink with no contact involved; the outermost-foot radius is (√7 / 2)-Lipschitz in the pose and a swing's Jacobian norm is at most 1 + √2, adopted here as floors for the allowances; the rigorous constants for the pushed motion are of the order e103·T (continuous law) and 10490·N (the finite solver), so a certificate by derivative bounds is not achievable for pushes; and a contact-free swing is exactly described in the pinned foot's coordinates, which is how the entry families are now computed (the clearance bound there, √3 R per radian, is rigorous). The stop window for a contact-free entry into the certified ball is about half a degree, which is why the report calls those families precise rather than practical.

Retro-mining depth. The trainer's retro-mining takes the final positions of lost games, steps back one move at a time, and asks every brain in the rated pool (57 to 67 faces, from the weakest ladder rung to the strongest net at three plies) to escape. Over nine recent jobs the seed families came out dead at 3, 3, 5, 9, 13, 14, 14, 14 and 15 moves before the end with nothing in the pool escaping; in one job a three-ply net escaped four moves from the end where the strongest two-ply net could not. These are population verdicts, not certificates — a brain none of them contains might still escape — but they put the engines' horizon well short of the game's.

Honest limits, third edition. Four dead points from real games is a small set; the mining found no position provably lost two moves before the end. The certified box around the one seed moves only the victim, with the attacker held; the two-piece ball being certified now will be a few tenths of a unit across, because the loss margin collapses on one side of the seed. The push law is validated against the engine at one substep phase; the engine's own outcome for a given stop depends slightly on how the piece is dragged (pushed poses differ by 0.04u at the median and 0.31u at worst between phases), and the certificates carry that as an allowance rather than a proof. Tubes of forced-win-in-three positions have been computed exactly but are thin; growing the dead regions is the open work.

Definitions and honest limits

perturbation
The ±6° per-piece rotation applied at the start of every game so the near-deterministic brains do not replay one fixed line.
fingerprint
An FNV-1a hash of a game's full move sequence (pivot, direction, stop angle to the nearest degree). Two games sharing a fingerprint played literally identically, the strongest possible signal of a forced line.
point of no return
The first turn after which the eventual winner's calibrated win chance stays at or above 90% to the end. It exists in only 0.86% of games (11 of 1,280).
calibration
The measured, not assumed, map from lead size to win chance, pooled over every position in every decided game.
certificate
A sampled claim about a region of positions: a grid of the throw margin, a slope allowance of three times the steepest measured slope over half a cell, one contact signature across the region, and an engine check of random poses. A check, not a theorem.
dead point
A position in which every legal move of the side to move, at every stopping angle, is followed by a certified throw. Four are known from real games.
throw margin
How far past the rim the victim’s outermost foot travels during the attacker’s best sweep, in board units; positive is a throw, and the certificates need it to exceed their allowance.

What this is and is not. Strong self-play is good evidence of depth, but it is the computer opponents playing themselves, not a human tournament. A fuller picture would add human games over time. The calibration pools positions across games and turns, so its buckets are not independent samples; it is a descriptive curve, not a fitted model. The opening-variety counts use a modest 24-game sample per level. The second-edition head-to-heads use 12-to-24-game samples per pairing: individually coarse, they are read for structure (orderings, cycles, trends) rather than precise rates, and every structural claim above rests on more than one pairing. All second-edition play used the current rules; the base tournament predates a crossing-rule tightening, discussed under the engine notes. And because Tau's state is continuous (any stop angle is legal), no amount of self-play can prove no forced win exists; it can only fail to find one across a wide, strong search, which is what happened here. The reproducible artifacts are the 1,280-game record, the 900+ second-edition games, the analysis scripts, and the labeled position datasets the fitted evaluations were learned from.
